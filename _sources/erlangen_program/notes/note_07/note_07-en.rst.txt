VII. On the Interpretation of Binary Forms
==========================================

.. rubric:: *A Comparative Review of Recent Researches in Geometry* — Note VII (Trans. :person:`M. W. Haskell <haskell>`, 1893, pp. 242–249)


We shall now consider the graphic illustration which can be given to the theory of invariants of binary cubics and biquadratics by taking advantage of the representation of :math:`x + iy` on the sphere.

A binary cubic :math:`f` has a cubic covariant :math:`Q`, a quadratic covariant :math:`\Delta`, and an invariant :math:`R` [#ep_u6_en46]_. From :math:`f` and :math:`Q` a whole system of covariant sextics

.. math::

   Q^2 + \lambda R f^2

may be compounded, among them being :math:`\Delta^3`. It can be shown [#ep_u6_en47]_ that every covariant of the cubic must resolve itself into such groups of six points. Inasmuch as :math:`\lambda` can assume complex values, the number of these covariants is :math:`\infty^2` (:person:`Beltrami <beltrami>`, *Memorie dell' Accademia di Bologna*, 1870).

The whole system of forms thus defined can now be represented upon the sphere as follows. By a suitable linear transformation of the sphere into itself let the three points representing :math:`f` be converted into three equidistant points of a great circle. Let this great circle be denoted as the equator, and let the three points :math:`f` have the longitudes :math:`0^\circ`, :math:`120^\circ`, :math:`240^\circ`. Then :math:`Q` will be represented by the points of the equator whose longitudes are :math:`60^\circ`, :math:`180^\circ`, :math:`300^\circ`; :math:`\Delta` by the two poles. Every form :math:`Q^2 + \lambda R f^2` is represented by six points, whose latitude and longitude are given in the following table, where :math:`\alpha` and :math:`\beta` are arbitrary numbers:

.. math::

   (\alpha, \beta), \quad (\alpha, 120^\circ + \beta), \quad (\alpha, 240^\circ + \beta), \quad (-\alpha, -\beta), \quad (-\alpha, 120^\circ - \beta), \quad (-\alpha, 240^\circ - \beta).

In studying the variation of these systems of points on the sphere, it is interesting to see how they give rise to :math:`f` and :math:`Q` (each reckoned twice) and :math:`\Delta` (reckoned three times).

A biquadratic :math:`f` has a biquadratic covariant :math:`H`, a sextic covariant :math:`T`, and two invariants :math:`i` and :math:`j`. Particularly noteworthy is the pencil of biquadratic forms :math:`i H + \lambda j f`, all belonging to the same :math:`T`, among them being the three quadratic factors into which :math:`T` can be resolved, each reckoned twice.

Let the centre of the sphere now be taken as the origin of a set of rectangular axes :math:`OX, OY, OZ`. Their six points of intersection with the sphere make up the form :math:`T`. The four points of a set :math:`i H + \lambda j f` are given by the following table, :math:`x, y, z` being the co-ordinates of any point of the sphere:

.. math::

   \begin{array}{rrr}
    x, &  y, &  z, \\
    x, & -y, & -z, \\
   -x, &  y, & -z, \\
   -x, & -y, &  z.
   \end{array}

The four points are in each case the vertices of a symmetrical tetrahedron, whose opposite edges are bisected by the co-ordinate axes; and this indicates the rôle played by :math:`T` in the theory of biquadratic equations as the resolvent of :math:`i H + \lambda j f`.

*ERLANGEN, October, 1872.*
