Part I: The Possibility of the Construction of Algebraic Expressions¶

Vorträge über ausgewählte Fragen der Elementargeometrie — I. Teil: Über die Möglichkeit der Konstruktion algebraischer Ausdrücke (1895 / 1897)

Part I, Ch. I: Algebraic Equations Solvable by Square Roots (1897 English)

1. Consider an algebraic expression $x$ capable of being constructed with straightedge and compasses from given segments. By our fundamental theorem (Introduction), $x$ is derived from known quantities—which, upon choosing a unit segment, we may treat as rational numbers (or rational parameters defining a domain of rationality)—by a finite number of rational operations and extractions of square roots.

Part I, Ch. II: The Delian Problem & Angle Trisection (1897 English)

1. Let us apply the general theorem established in Chapter I immediately to the celebrated Delian problem: the duplication of the cube. Legend relates that during a plague at Athens, the oracle of Apollo at Delos commanded the citizens to double the volume of his cubical altar while retaining its cubical shape.

Part I, Ch. III: The Division of the Circle into Equal Parts (1897 English)

1. The problem of dividing a given circle into $n$ equal parts has come down from antiquity; for more than two millennia mathematicians knew the possibility of solving it with straightedge and compasses only when $n = 2^h$, $3$, $5$, or the product of any two or three of these numbers (Euclid, Elements, Book IV).

Part I, Ch. IV: The Construction of the Regular Polygon of 17 Sides (1897 English)

1. Having seen in Chapter III that division of the circle into an odd prime number of equal parts by straightedge and compasses is possible only for Gauss's Fermat primes, we shall now show in an elementary way how to actually inscribe the regular polygon of $17$ sides in the unit circle.

Part I, Ch. V: General Considerations on Algebraic Constructions (1897 English)

1. We now lay aside constructions restricted to straightedge and compasses. Before leaving elementary methods altogether, however, we mention a new and remarkably simple geometric procedure: paper folding. Hermann Wiener showed how by folding paper one may obtain the planar nets of all the regular polyhedra (see W. Dyck, Katalog der Münchener mathematischen Ausstellung von 1893, Nachtrag, p. 52). Singularly, around the very same time an Indian mathematician, T. Sundara Row of Madras, published a suggestive little treatise, Geometrical Exercises in Paper Folding (Madras: Addison & Co., 1893), developing the same idea systematically and showing how to construct by points curves such as the ellipse, the cissoid, and others.
