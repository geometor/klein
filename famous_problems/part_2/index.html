Part II: Transcendence of the Numbers e and π¶

Vorträge über ausgewählte Fragen der Elementargeometrie — II. Teil: Die Transcendenz der Zahlen e und π (1895 / 1897)

Part II, Ch. I: Cantor’s Demonstration of Transcendental Numbers (1897 English)

1. Represent real numbers as usual by points along the axis of abscissas. If we restrict ourselves to rational numbers, the corresponding points fill the axis densely throughout (überall dicht)—in every interval, however small, infinitely many rational points lie. Nevertheless, as the ancients already discovered, the continuum of points on the line is not exhausted by rationals: between them enter the irrational numbers, and we must ask whether fundamental distinctions exist within the irrationals themselves.

Part II, Ch. II: Historical Survey of Efforts to Compute and Construct π (1897 English)

In Chapter IV we shall prove that $\pi$ belongs to the class of transcendental numbers whose existence Cantor established in Chapter I. That proof, first achieved by Ferdinand Lindemann in 1882 (Mathematische Annalen, Vol. XX), settled definitively a problem that had occupied mathematicians for nearly $4000$ years: the quadrature of the circle. Because $\pi$ is not algebraic, it certainly cannot be constructed with straightedge and compasses; quadrature of the circle in the classical sense is impossible.
